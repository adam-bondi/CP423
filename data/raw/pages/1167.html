    Graduate Courses

    We offer six courses each year. CS600 and CS601 are required courses. You must also register for CS695 or CS699 while working on your MRP or thesis.  Please see the academic calendar for more course information.

    You are allowed to take up to 1.0 credit from other graduate programs with approval from the graduate coordinator. In the past, Communication Studies master's students have taken graduate courses in programs such as Cultural Analysis and Social Theory, English and Film Studies, and Political Science.

    Special Topics Descriptions

    What is Communication Studies, anyway? In this course, we’ll explore the contested formation of the discipline, working across intellectual, contextual, and institutional dimensions. Given the scope of its topic, the course is necessarily selective in its coverage. We’ll start by considering efforts to delineate the idea of communication. Next, we’ll review broad surveys of the field, paying particular attention to absences or erasures in origin stories of Communication Studies. We’ll then explore examples of significant “turns” in Communication research – such as turns to infrastructure and mediatization – and examine shifts in the institutional sites of Communication Studies. Finally, we’ll address recent critical interventions in the discipline, including around #CommunicationSoWhite, de-Westernization, and climate crisis, reconsidering how earlier material in the course is implicated in these reckonings. The course will equip students with resources to navigate the discipline, locate their research interests within it, and appreciate the role of reflexivity in sustaining Communication Studies as a dynamic field.

    This course offers an introduction to Black feminist and womanist theories across geography, culture and time. To contextualize Black women’s experiences, class discussions will center Black women’s discourses and highlight how Black feminist thought shapes understandings of race, class, sexuality, and gender throughout the African diaspora. While informed by the histories and legacies of slavery and colonialism, Black Feminist Thought will take up questions of diaspora, nation and being, while challenging heteropatriarchy and other overlapping forms of social oppression.

    This graduate course examines how contemporary environmentalism is shaped by digital platforms and the political economies that sustain them. “Platform environmentalism” refers to the growing intersection of environmentalism and conservation with social media, apps, digital games, AI systems, and networked sensors to communicate, visualize, and govern ecological issues, as well as how these same technologies and infrastructures contribute to misinformation, extraction, carbon emissions, and new forms of environmental injustice. Through interdisciplinary readings in critical communication studies, new media studies, political ecology, and environmental humanities, the course explores topics such as the digital surveillance of nature, the material footprint and ecological impacts of cloud computing and AI; algorithmic conservation and remote sensing; the mediation of conservation and climate activism through social media platforms and the attention economy; and the aesthetics and impacts of producing, consuming, and engaging with digital and virtual natures.
